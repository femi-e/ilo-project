# Writing Style Guide

A personal style based on principles from ASD-STE100 Issue 9, adapted for general audience writing, cross referenced against plain language standards, and refined through iteration.

---

## Core Philosophy

Writing is a courtesy to the reader. The goal is to be understood on the first read without the reader having to go back. Clarity comes first, but not at the cost of sounding mechanical.

---

## Always Do These

### Active Voice

Make the subject of the sentence the thing doing the action. Active voice is shorter and removes ambiguity about who is responsible. [STE Rule 3.6] [U.S. Digital.gov: "Active voice makes it clear who should do what. It eliminates ambiguity about responsibilities."] [U.S. National Archives: "The active voice eliminates confusion by forcing you to name the actor in a sentence."]

The score was calculated from five dimensions.
We calculated the score from five dimensions.

The second version tells you who did it. The first makes you wonder "calculated by whom?"

### Short Sentences, Varied Length

Aim for an average of 15 to 20 words per sentence. Mix shorter and longer sentences to avoid a choppy rhythm. A block of sentences all the same length reads like a checklist, not prose. [Plain English Campaign: "Keep your sentences short" with a 15-20 word average.] [Plain Language Commission: "Write sentences that average 15-20 words."] [Gunning Fog Index: sentence length is a primary factor in readability.]

She walked into the room. She sat down. She opened her laptop. She started typing. (Choppy. Every sentence is the same length.)
She walked into the room and sat down. Then she opened her laptop and started typing. (Varied. Flows better.)

### One Term for One Thing

Don't rename things for variety. If you call it a "score," don't also call it a "rating" and an "index" in the same document. The reader will wonder if you mean three different things. Writers call this "elegant variation," and it causes confusion more often than it adds style. [STE Rule 1.11] [U.S. Digital.gov style guide: "Avoid noun strings... cut descriptive words that aren't essential."]

### Short Words Over Long Ones

Short words are easier to read and harder to misunderstand. Fancy vocabulary doesn't make you sound smarter. It makes the reader work harder. [Plain English Campaign A to Z of alternative words: provides plain alternatives to pompous vocabulary.] [Plain Language Commission: "Use everyday English."] [University of Kent plain English guide: "Use the simplest words that work."]

Use this method to find the best settings.
Utilize the subsequent methodology to ascertain the optimal configuration.

Both say the same thing. One is a courtesy.

### Context Before Action

When something depends on a condition, state the condition first. The reader needs to know when something applies before they can understand what applies. [STE Rule 5.4: "Write the condition first in the sentence, and then use a comma to show the end of the condition and the start of the instruction."]

Apply the primer when the surface is dry.
When the surface is dry, apply the primer.

The second version is easier to parse because you know the condition before you get to the action.

### Build Gradually

Start with the shape, then fill in the details. Each sentence should introduce one piece of the picture. Don't put everything in the first sentence. [STE Rule 6.1: "Give information gradually and make sure that each sentence contains only one subject."]

The index scores locations on five dimensions. Reputation, engagement, risk, root cause themes, and market position. Each one comes from a different data source.

### Be Specific

Abstract statements leave the reader guessing. Replace vague references with concrete details. [STE 5.1: "Be accurate. Do not give information that is not accurate or can have different meanings."]

Different temperatures will change the cure time.
When the temperature goes from 20°C to 30°C, the cure time drops from 4 hours to 2 hours.

The first version forces the reader to wonder "how much and in which direction?" The second answers those questions.

### Explain Why

Don't just state what to do. Tell the reader why it matters. The why is what makes information stick and what helps the reader remember it. [STE Rule 7.3: "Always tell your reader about the problems that can occur if the reader does not obey."]

Keep multi word nouns to a maximum of three words.
Keep multi word nouns to a maximum of three words. Longer ones confuse readers, especially people who don't speak English as a first language.

The second sentence is why the first sentence exists. Without it, the first sentence is just an arbitrary rule.

## Use in Moderation

### Contractions

Contractions make writing sound natural. They match how people speak. Avoid them only when you want extra weight or formality. [University of Toronto "Writing for the Public": "Contractions are acceptable."] [Paul Graham "Write Like You Talk": contractions are part of natural spoken language. Writing should sound like you, not like a formal document.]

I do not recommend this approach. (Formal, emphatic. Slows the reader down intentionally.)
I don't recommend this approach. (Natural, conversational. Faster to read.)

### Phrasal Verbs

Phrasal verbs (figure out, set up, look into) are natural in English. Use them freely for general audiences. For audiences that may not be fluent, or in formal documents, prefer the single word alternative. [APC "Writing in English for a Multilingual Audience": phrasal verbs can confuse non-native speakers; prefer single word alternatives for multilingual readers.] [Academic writing guides: single-word synonyms preferred for formal register, but general writing has no such restriction.]

We need to figure out the right approach.
We need to determine the right approach.

Both work. Pick based on who you're writing for.

### "-ing" Verb Forms

Use them when they're natural. Avoid stacking multiple "-ing" words in the same sentence. One per sentence is a reasonable ceiling. [STE Rule 3.5 restricts "-ing" forms heavily, but general English has no such restriction. This is a personal preference for clean writing.]

Natural: When you are doing this procedure, obey the safety precautions.
Stacked: He was considering starting running daily. (Three in one sentence. Feels awkward.)

### Perfect Tenses (has, had, will have)

Use them when you need to show sequence or completion. Don't use them for every verb. Simple past is usually cleaner. [STE Rule 3.4 bans auxiliary verb constructions entirely. General English accepts them, but they add words without adding clarity when overused.]

Unnecessary: The system has been tested and we have confirmed it works.
Better: We tested the system and confirmed it works.

Necessary: By the time we arrived, the system had already shut down. (The sequence matters here.)

### Hedging (might, could, may, possibly)

Use hedging when you're genuinely uncertain. Skip it when you're making a recommendation or stating a fact. Over hedging makes you sound unsure of everything. [SEC Plain English Handbook: avoid hedge words like "might" and "may" when you mean something definite.]

We might need to adjust the weights. (Genuine uncertainty about whether adjustment is needed.)
You could set weights manually. (Presenting an option, not expressing doubt.)
This could possibly maybe be the right approach. (Too much hedging. Sounds uncertain.)

### Semicolons

Use them only when two sentences are closely related enough that the connection is worth showing. A period works in most cases. [STE Rule 8.1 bans semicolons. General English accepts them. Use judgment.]

Period is fine: The system failed. We don't know why.
Semicolon works: The system failed; we suspect a power surge caused it.

## Rarely

### Sentence Fragments

Use them only for deliberate emphasis where a full sentence would kill the rhythm. One per document, not one per paragraph. [NN/G Nielsen Norman Group: sentence fragments can improve web readability when used intentionally.] [University of Toronto: "Sentence fragments can be acceptable, but don't overuse them."]

Where they work: The system failed. No warning. No error log. Just silence. (The fragments add weight to the sequence.)
Where they don't: I reviewed the data. Found some issues. Need to investigate. (Feels rushed and incomplete.)

### Em Dashes

Avoid them. Use commas or periods instead. If you find yourself reaching for an em dash, consider restructuring the sentence.

---

## Putting It Together

Before editing, write naturally. Paul Graham calls this "write like you talk" — get the ideas down in your natural voice first. The clean up comes after.

Then check each sentence. Can you make the verb active? Are you calling the same thing by different names? Did you hedge something you actually know? Do your paragraph openings all follow the same pattern? Read it aloud. If you stumble, your reader will too.

The goal isn't to follow every point here. It's to make sure every sentence earns its place and every reader understands it the first time.
